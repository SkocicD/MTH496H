\subsection{Triangulations of Polygons}
\input{figures/triangulations/polygons/convex_polygon.tex}

The simplest case of a triangulation comes from a polygon because there is already some information about how the points are connected.
The following definition will serve as a basis for the rest of the arguments about triangulating polygons.

\begin{definition}[Triangulation of a Polygon]
	A decomposition of a polygon into triangles by a maximal set of non-intersecting diagonals.
\end{definition}

In simpler terms, a triangulation breaks a polygon into triangular pieces by drawing segments between its vertices so that no two segments intersect.
The term 'maximal set' is used in the definition to ensure that there is no vertex of the polygon on the edge of any triangle.
This only turns out to matter if there are three colinear vertices, but we will assume that the vertices are in general position, so this case will never occur.

Before getting into the general case, let us look first at the triangulation of a convex polygon.
Based on Definition~\ref{def:convex_polygon}, any line drawn across it does not intersect any of its edges.
Using this fact, we can quickly see a way to triangulate any convex polygon, just start at one vertex and draw lines to every other vertex as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:triangulations.polygons.basic_example}.
It is easy to see how this technique can extend to any convex n-gon to create a triangulation with n-2 triangles.
It is important to note that there can be more than one way to triangulate a polygon, with another example shown in Figure~\ref{fig:triangulations.polygons.basic_example}.
In fact, we may prefer one triangulation over another based on the use case, which we will explore in the section on Delaunay triangulations.

A more interesting problem arises when you try to triangulate a shape that is nonconvex.
It was very clear how a convex polygon could be triangulated, but maybe we can construct a nonconvex polygon that has no valid triangulation, perhaps using jagged edges, or long winding sections.
This turns out to be impossible, but to prove this, we need the following lemma.

\input{figures/triangulations/polygons/diagonal.tex}
\begin{lemma}
	A diagonal exists in any polygon, $P$, with more than 3 vertices.
	\label{lem:diagonal}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	Take some vertex $V$ from $P$ and label its neighboring vertices $U$ and $W$ and draw a segment between them.
	If $\overline{UW}$ does not intersect any edge of $P$, then $\overline{UV}$ is a diagonal, and we are done.

	Otherwise, there exists at least one vertex of $P$ inside $\triangle UVW$.
	Imagine taking a line, $L$, parallel to $\overline{UW}$ through $V$.
	Now let $L$ sweep it towards $\overline{UW}$.
	At any moment along its path, $L$ intersects with $\overline{UV}$ and $\overline{VW}$ at $U'$ and $W'$ to create $\triangle VU'W'$.
	At first, $\triangle VU'W'$ is empty of vertices of $P$, but eventually as $L$ moves, some vertex $V'$ of $P$ will be on $\overline{U'W'}$.
	Since, $\triangle VU'W'$ is empty of vertices of $P$, it is also empty of edges of $P$, so it is guaranteed that $\overline{VV'}$ does not intersect any edge of $P$ and hence is a diagonal.
\end{proof}

From which, the following theorem follows closely,

\begin{theorem}
	Any polygon can be triangulated
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}  %This proof comes from computational geometry by springer
	This is an inductive proof with the base case being a polygon with 3 vertices.
	This base case is trivial a triangle fits the definition of a triangulation.
	The inductive hypothesis is that any polygon with $m$ or fewer vertices can be triangulated, and the goal is to show that $P$ with $n = m+1 > 3$ vertices can be triangulated.

	Consider a polygon $P$.
	By Lemma~\ref{lem:diagonal}, at least one diagonal exists, choose one such diagonal and call it $D$.
	Now divide $P$ into two polygons $P_1$ and $P_2$ such that any edge in $P$ is in either $P_1$ or $P_2$, and $D$ is in both $P_1$ and $P_2$.
	The total number of edges in $P_1$ and $P_2$ is equal to $n+2$ since $D$ is in each new polygon.
	Without loss of generality, suppose $P_1$ has fewer edges than $P_2$.
	Since $P_1$ is a polygon, it must have at least 3 edges, which means that $P_2$ can have at most $n + 2 - 3 = n-1 = m$ edges, both of which can be triangulated by the inductive hypothesis, so $P$ can be triangulated by recombining the triangulation of $P_1$ and $P_1$.
\end{proof}

This inductive proof guarantees the triangulation of any polygon, but it also outlines a simple algorithm to triangulate any polygon;
choose any vertex on the polygon, draw a diagonal, then continue for each of the polygons created by this diagonal until every polygon is a triangle.
I will skip a fully fledged description of this algorithm, because a more useful algorithm is explained in detail later.
Before moving on, we can still squeeze a little more out of this lemma by using it to find the number of triangles that are in the triangulation of a polygon.

\begin{theorem}
	The triangulation of a polygon with $n$ vertices has $n-2$ triangles.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	This proof is again inductive and is similar to the previous proof, with the base case that a polygon with 3 vertices has one triangle.
	Our hypothesis is that a polygon with $k$ vertices has a triangulation with $k-2$ triangles with $k\le m$, and we want to show that a triangulation with $n=m+1$ vertices has $m-1$ triangles in its triangulation.

	Consider polygon $P$ with $n$ vertices.
	$P$ can be divided in two by a diagonal to form $P_1$ and $P_2$.
	Suppose $P_1$ has $a$ vertices, and $P_2$ has $b$ vertices, so $a+b = n+2$ and $a, b \le m$.
	By hypothesis $P_1$ and $P_2$ can be triangulated with $a-2$ and $b-2$ triangles, respectively, so the total number of triangles is $a-2+b-2 = (a+b)-4 = n+2-4 = n-2$.
\end{proof}
